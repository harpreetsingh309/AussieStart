# Overview
A short, practical history of Australia — enough to follow a news bulletin, understand a public holiday, and answer the history questions on the citizenship test.

# Why this matters
Australians rarely explain their own history to newcomers, but they refer to it constantly: Anzac Day, the Federation, the White Australia policy, the referendum, the Republic debate. Knowing the outline saves you from nodding along blankly, and the same material appears in the citizenship test if you go on to apply.

# When to do it
Any time. Useful before your first Australia Day or Anzac Day, and essential if you are studying for citizenship.

# Requirements
Nothing.

# Step-by-step guide

## 1. Before 1788
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived here for upwards of 60,000 years, in hundreds of distinct nations with their own languages and laws. This is the overwhelming majority of human history on this continent. See the History & Culture guide on First Peoples and Country for detail.

Makassan traders from Sulawesi visited northern Australia for centuries before European arrival, trading with Yolŋu people. Dutch navigators charted parts of the coast from 1606 and called it New Holland. James Cook mapped the east coast in 1770 and claimed it for Britain.

## 2. Colonisation, 1788 onwards
The First Fleet arrived at Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788, establishing a British penal colony. Convict transportation continued until 1868, bringing around 160,000 people. Free settlers followed, and separate colonies were established across the continent.

For First Nations peoples this was dispossession: frontier violence, introduced disease, and removal from Country. This period is contested and actively discussed in Australia today — you will hear the phrase "frontier wars" and the term "truth-telling".

## 3. Gold, wool and a growing colony
The gold rushes of the 1850s transformed the colonies, tripling the population in a decade and drawing migrants from China, Europe and the Americas. The **Eureka Stockade** at Ballarat in 1854, a miners' rebellion over licence fees, is remembered as an early stand for democratic rights. Wool made the colonies wealthy — the phrase "riding on the sheep's back" comes from this era.

## 4. Federation, 1901
On 1 January 1901 the six colonies joined to form the Commonwealth of Australia, with a written Constitution and a federal parliament. The states kept substantial powers, which is why school, health, transport and driving rules still differ by state today — something you will notice constantly as a new arrival.

The new parliament also passed the Immigration Restriction Act, the basis of what became known as the **White Australia policy**, which restricted non-European migration. It was dismantled progressively through the 1950s and 60s and formally ended in 1973.

## 5. The wars
**Anzac Day, 25 April**, marks the 1915 landing at Gallipoli by the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps. The campaign failed, but it became central to national identity. Dawn services are held nationwide and the day is a public holiday; it is treated with genuine solemnity.

The Second World War shifted Australia's strategic focus from Britain to the United States. Darwin was bombed in 1942. **Remembrance Day**, 11 November at 11am, marks the end of the First World War with a minute's silence — not a public holiday, but widely observed.

## 6. Post-war migration
From 1945 Australia ran a large migration program, first from Britain and Europe, later from Asia, the Middle East, Africa and the Pacific. The country shifted from an explicitly British society to one where around half the population was born overseas or has an overseas-born parent, and more than 300 languages are spoken at home.

This is the direct reason Australia describes itself as multicultural, and why your own arrival is an entirely ordinary Australian story.

## 7. The modern era
- **1967 referendum** — over 90% voted yes on counting Aboriginal people in the census and Commonwealth law-making powers.
- **1972–75** — the Whitlam government introduced universal healthcare, free university and no-fault divorce, and was dismissed by the Governor-General in 1975, a constitutional crisis still argued over.
- **1975** — the Racial Discrimination Act.
- **1984** — Medicare established in its current form. *Advance Australia Fair* replaced *God Save the Queen* as the national anthem.
- **1992** — the Mabo decision recognised native title.
- **1999 referendum** — a proposal to become a republic was defeated; Australia remains a constitutional monarchy.
- **2008** — the National Apology to the Stolen Generations.
- **2017** — same-sex marriage legalised after a national postal survey.
- **2023** — the Voice referendum was not carried.

## 8. How the country is run today
Australia is a constitutional monarchy and a parliamentary democracy. The head of state is the monarch, represented by the Governor-General; the head of government is the Prime Minister. Federal parliament has two houses, the House of Representatives and the Senate.

**Voting is compulsory** for citizens aged 18 and over, at federal, state and most local elections, and you can be fined for not voting. This surprises many new arrivals. Elections are run by the Australian Electoral Commission and are held on Saturdays.

# Common mistakes
- Treating Australia Day as uncomplicated — for many it is a day of mourning, and saying so is a mainstream position
- Treating Anzac Day as a celebration; it is a day of remembrance
- Assuming Australia is a republic, or that the Prime Minister is the head of state
- Assuming federal rules apply everywhere — states differ on a great deal
- Forgetting that voting is compulsory once you become a citizen

# FAQs
**What are the national symbols?** The flag with the Union Jack, Commonwealth Star and Southern Cross; the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander flags; the green and gold national colours; the golden wattle as national flower; the kangaroo and emu on the coat of arms.

**Is the history on the citizenship test?** Yes. The test draws on the *Our Common Bond* booklet published by the Department of Home Affairs, which covers history, government, symbols and Australian values. Study the official booklet rather than a summary like this one.

**Why do public holidays differ between states?** Because the states existed first and kept their own powers at Federation. Melbourne closes for a horse race; other states do not.

**What is the Republic debate?** An ongoing argument about replacing the monarch as head of state with an Australian. The 1999 referendum failed, partly over how a president would be chosen. It resurfaces periodically.

# Useful terms
- **Federation**: The 1901 union of the six colonies into one nation
- **The Commonwealth**: The federal level of government
- **Terra nullius**: "Land belonging to no one" — the legal fiction overturned by Mabo in 1992
- **Referendum**: A national vote to change the Constitution; needs a majority of voters and a majority of states
- **Anzac**: Australian and New Zealand Army Corps
- **Preferential voting**: Australia's system of ranking candidates in order
