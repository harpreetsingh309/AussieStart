# Overview
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are the First Peoples of this continent, with cultures that have been continuous here for upwards of 60,000 years. Understanding this is part of settling in Australia, not an optional extra.

# Why this matters
You will encounter First Nations culture within days of arriving — an Acknowledgement of Country at the start of a university lecture or a council meeting, flags flying outside a town hall, place names in local languages, artwork in the airport you landed at. Knowing what these mean, and the difference between them, lets you take part respectfully instead of guessing.

# When to do it
Read this early. Nothing here is a task with a deadline, but it shapes how you understand everything else in this app.

# Requirements
Nothing but attention.

# Step-by-step guide

## 1. Know who you are talking about
Aboriginal peoples and Torres Strait Islander peoples are two distinct groups, not one. Torres Strait Islander peoples come from the islands between the tip of Queensland and Papua New Guinea, and have their own languages, histories and cultures.

Neither group is a single people. There are hundreds of distinct nations, each with its own language, laws and Country. Around 250 Australian languages were spoken at the time of British colonisation, along with 600 to 700 dialects. Many are being actively revived today.

## 2. Understand "Country"
Country is written with a capital C, and it means far more than land. It takes in the land, waters, sky, animals, plants, stories, songs and the people belonging to a place, and the relationships among all of them. People speak of being *on* Country or *caring for* Country. When someone says "this is Wurundjeri Country" or "Yugambeh Country", they are naming the people whose Country you are standing on.

## 3. Learn whose Country you live on
Find out which nation's Country your suburb sits on — your local council website usually says, and AIATSIS publishes a map of Indigenous Australia. Some widely known examples: Sydney's city centre is Gadigal Country, Melbourne's is Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung and Boon Wurrung Country, Brisbane's is Turrbal and Yuggera Country, Perth's is Whadjuk Noongar Country, Adelaide's is Kaurna Country. Boundaries are complex and sometimes shared, so use the map as a starting point rather than a final answer.

## 4. Tell a Welcome from an Acknowledgement
A **Welcome to Country** can only be given by a Traditional Owner of that particular Country, or someone they have authorised. It is a formal protocol with deep roots in granting safe passage to visitors, and it may include speech, song, dance or a smoking ceremony.

An **Acknowledgement of Country** can be offered by anyone, including you. It is a short statement recognising the Traditional Owners of the place you are on and their continuing connection to it. A commonly used general form is:

> I acknowledge the Traditional Owners of the land on which we meet today, and pay my respects to Elders past and present.

If you know whose Country you are on, name them — it is more meaningful than the general version.

## 5. Use respectful language
The Australian Government Style Manual gives clear guidance. Prefer **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples** or **First Nations people**. Name the specific nation where you can — that is always better than a general term. Capitalise Country, Elders, Traditional Owners, Traditional Custodians, Acknowledgement of Country and Welcome to Country.

Avoid **Aborigines**, **Islanders**, the abbreviation **ATSI**, and possessive phrasings like "our Aboriginal people". Avoid the past tense in ways that imply these cultures are finished — they are living cultures. Avoid calling spiritual beliefs "myths" or "legends".

**Indigenous Australians** is common but flattens hundreds of distinct peoples into one label, so it is best kept for genuinely general contexts, and always capitalised.

## 6. Recognise the flags and the dates
The Aboriginal flag (black, red, yellow) and the Torres Strait Islander flag (green, blue, white and black with a white dhari headdress and star) are both official flags of Australia and you will see them flown alongside the national flag.

Dates you will hear about:
- **26 January** — the national public holiday. Many First Nations people and others mark it as Invasion Day or Survival Day, and attend rallies rather than celebrations. Expect a live national debate about the date; it is normal for people to hold strong views either way.
- **National Reconciliation Week**, 27 May to 3 June — bookended by the 1967 referendum and the Mabo decision.
- **NAIDOC Week**, usually in July — celebrating First Nations history, culture and achievements. Public events are open to everyone.
- **National Sorry Day**, 26 May — remembering the Stolen Generations.

## 7. Know the recent history you will hear referenced
- **1967 referendum** — over 90% voted yes to count Aboriginal people in the census and let the Commonwealth make laws for them.
- **Mabo, 1992** — the High Court rejected the doctrine that Australia was *terra nullius*, land belonging to no one, and recognised native title.
- **The Apology, 2008** — the Prime Minister apologised in Parliament to the Stolen Generations, the children forcibly removed from their families under past government policies.
- **Uluru Statement from the Heart, 2017** — a statement from First Nations delegates calling for a Voice to Parliament, treaty-making and truth-telling.
- **The Voice referendum, 2023** — a proposal to recognise First Peoples in the Constitution through an advisory Voice to Parliament. It was not carried; around 60% voted no, and it did not pass in any state.

# Common mistakes
- Assuming "Aboriginal" covers Torres Strait Islander peoples as well — it does not
- Treating these as ancient history rather than living cultures and current politics
- Offering a "Welcome to Country" yourself — an Acknowledgement is the one anyone can give
- Buying "Aboriginal-style" art or souvenirs that are not made by First Nations artists; look for community-owned art centres and the Indigenous Art Code
- Photographing ceremonies, artwork or sacred sites where signs ask you not to
- Asking someone to speak for all First Nations people, or asking how much Aboriginal ancestry they have

# FAQs
**As a migrant, is this my history too?** You are joining a country where it is a live and present issue. You are not being asked to carry blame for the past. You are being asked to know where you are and to be respectful about it.

**Do I have to do an Acknowledgement of Country?** No. It is not compulsory anywhere. If you chair a meeting or open an event, it is common practice and welcomed when done sincerely.

**Why do some places have two names?** Many placenames are being restored in local languages, sometimes as dual names — Uluru / Ayers Rock, K'gari / Fraser Island, though the latter has now formally reverted to K'gari alone. Use the First Nations name where it is the official one.

**Where can I learn more properly?** Look for events run by First Nations organisations, local land councils and keeping places. Cultural tours led by Traditional Owners are widely available and are the best way to learn on Country.

# Useful terms
- **Country**: Land, waters, sky and all the relationships and responsibilities tied to a place
- **Traditional Owners / Traditional Custodians**: The people belonging to a particular Country
- **Elder**: A person recognised by their community for knowledge and standing; often addressed as Aunty or Uncle
- **Mob**: An informal term for one's people or community, used by First Nations people
- **Native title**: Legal recognition of continuing rights to land under traditional law and custom
- **Stolen Generations**: First Nations children forcibly removed from their families under past government policies
